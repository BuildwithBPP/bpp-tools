Webflow Client Hosting SOP
Who pays for the site plan, when, and how the client gets edit access
← Back to Delivery
The rule
BPP never fronts a client's website hosting. The client's card goes on the site plan before the custom domain is ever connected.
Written after the HALO Commons engagement, where BPP paid the Basic annual plan as a convenience. That one shortcut cost two working sessions, put the client in front of a $180 charge they believed they had already paid, and stranded a prepaid year in the wrong workspace.

The distinction everyone gets wrong

Two different Webflow charges get confused constantly. Keep them separate in your head and most of this SOP becomes obvious.

WhatWho it belongs toWhat it does
Workspace planBPP (Freelancer tier)Lets BPP build client sites, transfer them, and use Client Payments. Paid by BPP, always. This is a cost of doing business.
Site planThe clientThe recurring fee that keeps one site live on its custom domain. Basic is roughly $180/yr. This is the client's cost, always.

Upgrading the workspace does not make hosting free, and a site plan does not travel with a site transfer. Someone always pays Webflow to keep a site live. The only question this SOP answers is who, and how it gets set up so nobody pays twice.

Standard path: new engagements

  1. Name the number in the proposal, not at launch. Hosting is out of scope in the BPP website agreement. Put "hosting approximately $180/yr, billed to you directly" in the SOW so the request at launch is a formality instead of a surprise fee.
  2. Repeat it at kickoff with a real figure. $180/yr works out to about $15/mo billed annually. The domain is separate and small, usually around $20/yr at the registrar.
  3. Build on the free staging subdomain. No site plan is needed while the site is on *.webflow.io. Do not buy a plan to build.
  4. When the site is client-approved and ready to go live, send the payment request. Webflow dashboard → Client management → Clients → Send a payment request. Select the site, send it to the client's email.
  5. Client accepts, creates a free Webflow account, adds their card. The site plan activates on their card. BPP's card never touches it.
  6. Now connect the custom domain and publish. Domain connection requires an active site plan, which is why this step comes last.
  7. Separately, add the client as a content editor. Paying for hosting and having edit access are two different toggles. Granting one does not grant the other.

Remediation path: BPP already paid

If a site plan is already on BPP's card, do not send a payment request mid-cycle to fix it. Read the order summary carefully before sending anything. Webflow's own wording on that screen confirms the trap:

What the screen saysWhat it actually means
"You will be credited for unused time remaining on the existing subscription"The leftover value goes back to BPP as Webflow account credit. Not to the client, and not as cash.
"We'll charge the client $180.00 ... minus existing credits on their account"The client is charged the full amount today. BPP's credit does not reduce their bill.
"Billed every year starting from the date the client makes the payment"The renewal date resets to today, so the client pays for months already covered.

Do this instead: give the client a free content-editor seat now so they can update their own site, then send the Client Payments request shortly before the existing plan renews. At that point the prepaid year is consumed, the credit is near zero, and the client's card cleanly picks up the next year. No double charge, no stranded money.

Timing is the whole game. The request has to go out before the renewal date, not after. Miss it and BPP's card gets charged for another full year and you are right back where you started. Put the renewal date on a calendar the day you identify it.

Edit access vs. ownership transfer

When a client says "I want to be able to update my own site," they almost never mean "I want to own the Webflow account." Pick the smaller tool.

ToolUse whenWhat happens
Content editor seatThe client wants to update text and images. This is the default.Free. Site stays in BPP's workspace, BPP keeps design control, our guides still apply. Scope the seat to that one site so they cannot see other clients' work.
Client PaymentsThe client should pay for their own hosting, which is every engagement.Site stays in BPP's workspace. Only the billing moves. Requires BPP on Freelancer or Agency.
Full site transferThe client must legally hold the account in their own name. Rare.Ownership moves to their workspace and BPP loses design control. Any prepaid time is stranded. If you must do it, do it at renewal.

What not to do

Never take a client's card details verbally. Not on a call, not on a recorded call, not read aloud in chunks. On the HALO engagement the founders dictated a live card number, expiry, and CVC during a recorded working session, and it had to be manually scrubbed from the archived transcript. Client Payments exists precisely so the client enters their own card and BPP never handles it.
Never put the client's card on BPP's workspace as a payment method. That is a different action from Client Payments and it scopes their card to the whole workspace instead of one site.
Never buy a site plan "just to get started." Build on the free staging subdomain. The plan is only required to connect a custom domain, and by then the client should be paying.

Client-facing script

Naming the cost at kickoff:

"Hosting runs about $180 a year, which works out to $15 a month if you pay annually. That's billed to your card directly, not through me. The account goes in your name so you own it outright. I'm not holding your website hostage, and you're not waiting on me to pay a bill. The domain is separate and small, usually around $20 a year at the registrar."

If they push back on the price:

"That's the same range as Wix or Squarespace, so you're not paying a premium. The difference is what we can build. Those platforms are template-based and they cap how far you can customize because you can't get under the hood. Here we're not fighting a template, so your site looks like your business instead of looking like everybody else's."
