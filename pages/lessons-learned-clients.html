SEEDFOLKids
Client
Brand + Strategic Planning ($4,200) · full brand, web, store, CRM and GBP buildout · closed Jun 2026 · Full review →
1
Lock an itemized deliverable list in the proposal. Scope grew from brand work into a full web, store, CRM, and CFO buildout with no change to the $4,200 value. Itemize every deliverable up front so additions stay visible and billable.
2
Design for client independence from day one. This client will not self-update, and the site-management guide stayed blocked. Bake low-maintenance structure into the build and make a handoff guide plus training a gated closeout step.
3
Map the real decision-makers early. Jeannette defers operational calls to Diana. Route those approvals to Diana from the start so deferred decisions stop stalling deliverables.
4
Model fluid products as "core + seasonal." The honey flavors changed constantly and broke the site and GBP sync. A fixed core list with seasonal callouts is the reusable pattern for any product-fluid client.
5
Keep the board current as you go. Closeout statuses were bulk-updated at the end, so mid-project status was not trustworthy. Move status the moment work changes.
6
Lock decision-maker roles in writing before design starts; post-sign-off changes are a paid extension. Jeannette was the sole design approver all project, but at closeout Diana surfaced and rejected most of it (the brand yellow, the honey-store layout, and the core-plus-seasonal flavor model she had earlier agreed to). It cost a week-long extension, a full honey-store rebuild, and a photo redo. Name the real approvers and their authority up front; after sign-off, changes are billable.
7
Make operational process-mapping a standard early step, and an offering. Across clients, not just this one: map operations, accountability, and the real decision-makers at kickoff so the right approvers are in the room from day one instead of surfacing at closeout. Helps the client organize and protects our scope.
Lois Marketing
Client
Operator System ($4,500) · process mapping, marketing playbook, HubSpot buildout, margin dashboard, website and one AI automation · closed Aug 2026 · Full review →
1
Gate every AI workspace setup behind a technical readiness check. The client had no free storage, no RAM headroom and no active membership, and the session ran anyway. It turned a productized setup into hours of unbilled labor. Verify device specs, free disk and an active subscription before the session ever goes on a calendar.
2
Teach what a local file is before teaching what the system does, then hand over a "do not delete" sheet. The client deleted his entire workspace folder believing it was a leftover download artifact. It surfaced only at closeout, during a rabbit chase over a skill that would not run, and cost roughly 35 minutes of explanation on top of the troubleshooting. Nothing had ever told him the folder was the product.
3
Book every meeting at kickoff. Client-side latency was the top driver of the 6-out-of-10 self-rating, and it was structural rather than personal. This owner respects a calendar invite and leaves texts and email unread for days. Send the full meeting set and take availability before the kickoff call ends.
4
Technical delivery needs a technical setting, and the technical seat is not delegable. A session where the decision-maker is distracted and hands the laptop to an unprepared team member is not a working session. Reschedule it. Pushing through produces a client who cannot use what he bought, which is exactly what happened.
5
Sell the retainer at proposal, not at closeout. An extra $250 on an $8,000 package is invisible while the client is already saying yes. The same $250 asked at the close is a fresh decision made after the buying energy is gone, and it forces us to switch from delivery mode into sales mode in front of them. Already corrected on the HALO Pathways quote.
+ also: pre-brief internally before client-facing action-plan calls · move Monday status as work changes, this is the second engagement in a row where the board could not be trusted mid-flight · scope and price the workspace setup as a real deliverable · Skills, not prompt engineering, is the 2026 training and lead-magnet play · give written termination notice whenever a contract calls for one.
Halo Commons · HALO Pathways
Client
Nonprofit business plan + web + GBP buildout · two entities (Halo Commons LLC for-profit + HALO Pathways 501(c)(3)) · closed Aug 2026 · Full review →
1
Confirm the IRS 501(c)(3) determination letter before promising nonprofit tech perks and before making any public tax-status claim. Google for Nonprofits, TechSoup, and the other perks verify against the IRS federal tax-exempt databases, not state records. HALO Pathways had Florida nonprofit incorporation plus an EIN but no confirmed IRS determination letter, so the Google for Nonprofits setup stalled mid-flight. State incorporation + EIN is not federal exemption. The second, more expensive consequence: a false "registered 501(c)(3)" claim and the EIN sat on a live public donation page until the board ordered every donation surface stripped on 2026-07-14. Soliciting in or from Florida also triggers an FDACS charitable-solicitation registration duty regardless of IRS status. Correct sequence: state formation + EIN → file Form 1023/1023-EZ → determination letter → then perks, donation surfaces, and any public claim.
2
Sell the Form 1023 filing as a paid add-on for every 501(c)(3) client. Federal 501(c)(3) status requires filing IRS Form 1023 (or 1023-EZ) and receiving the determination letter, which is a real prerequisite for all the nonprofit tech perks. The IRS process is slow (weeks to months) and an attorney charges far more than BPP needs to, so scoping the filing up front is both a genuine value-add for the client and a margin line for us. Turn the blocker we hit here into a standard billable service.
3
Never front a client's website hosting. Put their card on it from day one. BPP paid HALO's Webflow Basic annual site plan (~$180/yr) to get halocommons.com connected. That single convenience cost two full working sessions to untangle, produced a client-facing moment where HALO was asked to pay $180 they believed they had already paid, and left a prepaid year stranded in the wrong workspace. It also meant taking the founders' card details verbally on a recorded call, which is a PCI exposure we should never repeat. The fix is procedural, not clever: the client pays for hosting directly, in their own name, before the domain is ever connected. Full click-path in the Webflow Client Hosting SOP →
4
A prepaid site plan does not travel with a site transfer, and "transfer" is usually the wrong tool anyway. Two separate things get confused constantly: the workspace plan (BPP's Freelancer tier, which enables transfers) and the site plan (the recurring fee that keeps one site live on its domain). Upgrading the workspace does not make hosting free, and transferring a site strands whatever prepaid time is left behind in the origin workspace. When the goal is "the client can update their own site," the answer is a free content-editor seat, not a transfer. Reserve an actual ownership transfer for when the client must legally hold the account, and time it at renewal so nothing prepaid is thrown away.
5
Dual-entity nonprofits need their own package tier. Launch Pad prices one business plan. HALO needed two firewalled plans, one for HALO Commons LLC and one for HALO Pathways 501(c)(3), which roughly doubled the plan effort and was absorbed for a ~$200 credit. Price the structure, not just the deliverable count, and build a nonprofit tier that carries the extra compliance work with it.
+ also: lock the legal entity name from the filed documents before any asset carries it · budget the revision window into the original timeline, sized to the deliverable · never collect payment details on a call.
