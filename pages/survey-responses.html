Notes

I'm very spread thin. I do a little bit of everything — the internal sprints bucket is by far my biggest, because that's where I'm building things for the team (plugins, MCPs, hub, Monday admin, anything that makes the rest of the business run cleaner). The pattern is that I keep absorbing “make this work” tasks instead of distributing them.

The reason the total varies is that internal sprints flex based on what's in the build pipeline. Standing meetings, client meetings, and financial work stay roughly constant. Build time is the volume knob.

The reason I'm running this whole exercise is to figure out how to delegate more or automate more. The current state isn't sustainable as we add more clients or more tooling, and the team deserves a structure that doesn't depend on me being the systems person for every function.

Where ownership should be clearer

Two levels:

3. Energy

Energizers (top 3)
  1. Helping clients actually win. Watching them earn more, grow, save time from the tools we put in their hands. That refills me.
  2. Sitting with AI to brainstorm and build systems that reduce friction. Process optimization, automation, dashboards. When I can see top-down how things connect, I get energy. When the business is spaghetti, I have to decode before I can even start, and that drains.
  3. In-person team time. Whiteboard / strategy sessions, content days, team outings, networking events. The team time is its own category of fuel.
Drainers (top 3)
  1. Brand / visual / logo design work. I'm not a designer and shouldn't be doing this. Belongs with Eli.
  2. Discovery calls when someone else from the team is already the lead. If Eli or Kenny owns the deal, my presence is redundant.
  3. Untangling spaghetti. Decoding unstructured work before I can start contributing — the friction tax I pay when systems aren't documented.

Also draining: procrastination — when something I should have shipped is lingering, it sits on me even when I'm not actively working on it.

Would want to own

Largely already doing what I want to own: business process optimization, AI infrastructure (co-owned is fine), the operational backbone — making sure the business runs smoothly, top-down.

What I'd actively want to NOT own: HubSpot, Marketing long-term, Brand visual.

4. Where it's unclear

Self scope creep

The underlying pattern: I think about the business at a high level, see gaps, and fill them myself instead of stopping to delegate. That's the engine behind almost every scope-creep example.

  • QC by default. I become the quality gate even when no one made me the QC owner.
  • Brand visual / logo / branding kit. I want to delegate this.
  • Marketing strategy beyond initial direction. Vendor / contractor management. Tool stack removal. AI tooling decisions made solo.

The fix isn't a personality fix (“stop seeing gaps”). It's a mechanism: when I see a gap, the move should be flag it for the team to take, not silently absorb it.

Other scope creep

I don't see scope creep on others. Eli owns Monday — that's clearly his. The rest, I genuinely don't have an answer for. Worth checking against the other surveys.

Decisions stuck (three mechanisms)
  1. Validation-seeking. The team isn't always comfortable making a decision without checking with someone first — sometimes specifically with me, because I'll disagree or have a different way of doing it.
  2. No clear ownership. When no one knows whose call it is, the decision sits.
  3. QA / QC bottleneck. Decisions wait on review when the QA function doesn't have an owner.
Stepped back

Stepping back isn't actually my pattern. I lean in.

The more honest read: I tend to step IN on things others technically own — helping out with HubSpot, helping out with the website, stepping in on calls. The pattern: I have the data/automation/systems instinct, so when I see something I think should be done differently, I weigh in.

The fix isn't “step back more” — it's calibrate the timing. Let the owner lead first.

5. Stop / Start / Continue

  • STOP: Working in silos. Compound our efforts and protect transparency between us.
  • START: Utilizing AI as an employee and automations to fill our gaps and save our time. The bridge between “we don't have budget for a hire” and “we can't keep doing it ourselves.”
  • CONTINUE: Our check-ins and team outings. The cadence of staying connected is the thing that keeps trust intact when we're under load.

9. Free space

The team has been doing excellent work with the bandwidth and tools we have. Three co-owners running BPP alongside full-time day jobs and still shipping is not a small thing.

I'd like to continue shaping BPP into something all 3 of us actually want to do. The chart should describe the BPP we're building toward, not just the BPP we have today.

Side missions and BPP can be allies, not competitors. Each of us has personal endeavors and side projects. Some of that energy can land inside BPP — building tools, products, processes that ALSO scratch the personal-build itch. The AI plugin work is the cleanest example. BPP shouldn't take away from personal stuff, but it should benefit from the parts of personal energy that align with where we're going.

Long-term: less time-for-sale, more leverage. Repeatable and product-like, not always service-based. The AI workforce thesis is one piece. Productized offerings are another. The chart should hold both today's accountability and tomorrow's leverage.
