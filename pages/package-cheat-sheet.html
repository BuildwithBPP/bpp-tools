BPP Sales Playbook

Everything you need to sell a BPP package: what we actually build under the hood, how to explain it to an owner who is not technical, and how to break down the value so the price makes sense. Read the first two sections before your first call.

Part 1 · What we actually sell, technically

You cannot sell this well until you understand what is really being built. Clients call it "the AI stuff." Internally it is five components. Most packages are some subset of these, sized to the business.

1. A structured file and folder system. Their documents, templates, and data organized in one consistent hierarchy inside the tools they already pay for (Google Drive, OneDrive, SharePoint, Teams). This sounds boring and it is the single most important piece. AI cannot help a business whose information is scattered. Neither can a new employee. Everything else sits on top of this.

Sell this harder than you think you should. Almost every business we talk to has scattered files from day one. It is the most common problem we see and the easiest one for an owner to recognize in themselves, which makes it our most reliable way into a conversation. Ask where their files live and most owners will admit the mess before you have to point it out.

Platform check, ask this early. Most clients are already on Google Drive, Microsoft Teams, or OneDrive, and we build inside whatever they have rather than moving them. If they are not on any of them, standing that up is step zero and it adds time to the engagement, so catch it during discovery and scope it rather than discovering it in week one. The upside: a business with nothing set up is often the easiest to help, because there is no bad structure to undo.

2. AI skills. Saved, reusable instruction sets that turn a repeatable business task into a one-command operation: write the proposal, draft the follow-up, summarize the meeting, build the weekly report. We write these against the client's real process and their real documents, so the output sounds like them and follows their steps. Think of a skill as a trained employee who never forgets the process and never has an off day.

3. Automations. Connections between their tools so information moves without a person re-typing it. A form fills, the CRM record is created, the follow-up email schedules itself, the task appears on someone's list. Multi-step and cross-tool.

4. A CRM. The system of record for leads and customers, so follow-up is tracked instead of remembered. We either set up HubSpot fresh or audit and fix whatever they already have.

5. A dashboard. Pulls numbers out of those systems into one auto-refreshing view: revenue, pipeline, margin by service, cash flow. Built in Power BI or Looker Studio.

Why this actually makes them money (know this chain cold)

1.
Scattered information means nobody can find anything, AI cannot help, and work gets redone. That is pure lost hours.
2.
Organized information plus skills means the repeatable work takes minutes instead of hours. The owner's most expensive hours get handed back.
3.
A CRM means follow-up actually happens. Most small businesses lose more deals to slow follow-up than to price. Closing even one more deal a month usually pays for the whole engagement.
4.
A dashboard means they can finally see which services make money. Owners routinely discover they have been selling something unprofitable for years.
5.
SOPs and automation mean the business stops depending on the owner's memory, which is what lets them grow without hiring another manager.
The one-sentence version: fewer dropped leads means more deals closed, faster admin means more hours to sell, and clear numbers mean they stop leaking money they cannot see. Organized businesses make more and keep more.

Part 2 · How to run the conversation

Open on money or time, never on AI. "Most businesses your size are leaking money they cannot see. Slow follow-up, no system, no clear read on what is actually profitable. That is usually where we start."
Ask, then shut up. Use the discovery questions below. You are listening for one sentence you can repeat back to them later. Do not pitch yet.
Name their problem in their own words. "So it sounds like everything runs through you, and when you are busy the follow-ups just stop." Let them confirm before you go further.
Now name the package and explain it plainly. Use the package block below: the Say this line, then the "how does that work" explanation. Stay out of the plumbing.
Break it down piece by piece. Walk them through what they get and tie every piece to an outcome. This is the part that makes the price feel fair. Never list deliverables without the "so that" attached.
Anchor the price against the leak, not against nothing. "One extra deal a month covers this." Price sounds big in a vacuum and reasonable next to the problem.
If they hesitate on a big build, drop to the Jumpstart Pilot tier. Proof beats persuasion. Never discount the big package to win the deal, step them down to the Pilot tier instead.

Discovery questions that surface the money

On follow-up and lost deals: "When someone reaches out, what happens next? Who owns it? How do you know if nobody followed up?"  ·  "How many deals do you think slip because nobody got back to them in time?"

On the owner as bottleneck: "What falls apart when you take a week off?"  ·  "What is the thing only you know how to do?"  ·  "How much of your week goes to work someone else could do if it were written down?"

On visibility: "Which of your services is the most profitable?" (If they hesitate, that is the dashboard sale.)  ·  "How do you know how you did last month? How long does that take to pull together?"

On where things actually live (ask this on every call): "Where do your files live right now? Google Drive, Teams, someone's desktop?"  ·  "If I asked you for last year's contract with your biggest client, how long would that take?"  ·  "Does everyone save things the same way, or does each person have their own system?"
Why this one matters: the answer is almost always messy, which hands you the problem in their own words. It also tells you whether platform setup is step zero, which you need to know before you quote.

On the repetitive work: "What do you or your team retype more than once?"  ·  "How long does a proposal take to get out the door?"

On readiness: "Have you tried fixing this before? What happened?" (Tells you if they are a Pilot-tier candidate or ready for a full build.)

Part 4 · Objections you will actually hear

"Can't I just use ChatGPT myself?"
"You can, and honestly you should. But ChatGPT on its own does not know your business, your pricing, your process, or where your documents live, so you get generic answers and you end up rewriting them. The value is not the AI, it is the system around it. That is the difference between a tool you occasionally use and something that actually takes work off your plate. If you want to start there, our Jumpstart coaching teaches you exactly that."
"That is expensive."
Never defend the number in a vacuum. Put it next to the leak or next to a hire. "What would one more closed deal a month be worth?" or "An ops manager is $70K a year, every year. This is once." Then go quiet and let them do the math.
"We already have a CRM."
"Good, we are not going to rip it out. We audit what you have and make it actually work, same price either way. Most of the time the problem is not the software, it is that nobody set it up around how you actually sell."
"Will my team actually use it?"
"That is the right question, and it is why we map your process first instead of installing software first. We build it around how your people already work, we train them on it, and we leave written playbooks behind. The stuff that fails is the stuff that gets installed and explained once."
"Is my data safe? Where does it go?"
"We build inside your accounts, on tools you already own. You own everything, and you keep it if you ever stop working with us. We are not holding your business hostage in our system."
"How long before I see something?"
Give a real answer: the Pilot tier is two weeks, Operator is 6 to 8, Scale is 8 to 12. Then add the honest part: "You will see the process map in the first couple of weeks, and that alone usually changes how you think about the business." If they need proof before committing, that is exactly what the Jumpstart Pilot tier is for.
"We're not really set up on anything. Our files are all over the place."
This is not an objection, it is the sale. Do not react like it is a problem. "That is the most common thing we see, and honestly it is easier to fix than a bad system somebody already built." Then get concrete: find out whether they are on Google Drive, Teams, or OneDrive. If they are on one of them we build inside it. If they are on none of them, say plainly that getting them set up is the first step and that it adds a bit of time, then scope it. Never quote a package before you know this answer.
"We tried something like this before and it did not stick."
This is a buying signal, not a rejection. "What happened?" Listen for the real reason, which is almost always that someone installed tools without fixing the process, or nobody was trained. Then point at the Pilot tier: "Then do not take my word for it. Let's do one thing in two weeks and you can judge us on that."
