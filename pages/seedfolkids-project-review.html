Honest read. SEEDFOLKids was contracted as a $4,200 brand and strategic-planning engagement, but the work delivered was a full brand-plus-systems buildout: brand kit, a live Wix site, an online store with working checkout, a donation flow, a HubSpot CRM, and a Google Business Profile, with QuickBooks/CFO cleanup running in parallel. The build shipped and the site is live. Two items remain in flight at closeout: the QuickBooks/CFO cleanup and the client-independence handoff. Closeout also ran about a week long because the client's operations lead surfaced late and rejected much of the already-approved design (see lessons 6 and 7). The gap between the contracted scope and what we actually delivered is the central lesson below.

Retrospective · 4Ls
Liked · Learned · Lacked · Longed-for
Internal team reflection. Honest read, not marketing copy.
Liked
One engagement, many disciplines. Brand, web, store, SEO, GBP, CRM, and donations all shipped from a single project.
The site went live and works. Store checkout and the donation flow are functional on the live domain.
The strategy was sharp. "An identity program disguised as a garden program" gave every downstream choice a center. The client liked the direction.
The logo iterated cleanly to a yes. Leaf-as-i integration and palette fixes landed with the founder.
Learned
This is a committee-decision client. Jeannette defers operational calls (marketing format, honey flavors) to Diana.
The client will not self-maintain the site. Jeannette was firm she does not want to do weekly updates herself.
A fluid product breaks the site. Honey flavors change constantly, so the site and Google profile drift out of sync.
The named approver was not the real one. Jeannette was the sole design approver all project, but Diana was the de facto authority and stayed out of design until closeout, then rejected most of it.
Lacked
A locked, itemized scope up front. The work grew from brand into a full systems build with no matching change to the deal.
A finished client-independence handoff. The basic site-management guide stayed blocked through closeout.
An early decision owner for operations. Cards-vs-flyers and flavor calls sat deferred, and the business cards never started.
Longed-for
A standing post-launch maintenance plan. Something that keeps the site and GBP current without leaning on the client.
Approvals routed to the real decision-maker. Getting Diana into the loop earlier on operational calls.
Scope priced to reality. A buildout this broad should carry a number closer to the work.
Action Plan · DAKI
Drop · Add · Keep · Improve
Each Add and Improve carries an owner and a "before the next engagement" trigger.
Drop
Assuming the client will keep their own site updated. Build for low maintenance instead of handing over a site that needs weekly attention.
Add
A gated client-independence handoff: a site-management guide plus a live training, required before any project closes. Owner: Eli + Kenny
A kickoff process-mapping step that names operations, accountability, and the real decision-makers in writing. Offer it as a service that organizes the client and protects our scope. Owner: Eli
Keep
Brand-strategy-first sequencing and the milestone structure. Strategy anchored every later decision and the milestones kept a broad build legible.
Improve
Keep the Monday board current during the project, not bulk-updated at closeout, and lock an itemized scope in the proposal. Owner: full team

Lessons We Carry Forward
Also logged in Client Lessons Learned
These mirror SEEDFOLKids' entry in the cumulative Client Lessons Learned log →
1
Lock an itemized deliverable list in the proposal. Scope grew from brand work into a full web, store, CRM, and CFO buildout with no change to the $4,200 value. Itemizing every deliverable up front keeps additions visible and billable.
2
Design for client independence from day one. This client will not self-update, and the site-management guide stayed blocked. Bake low-maintenance structure into the build and make a handoff guide plus training a gated closeout step.
3
Map the real decision-makers early. Jeannette defers operational calls to Diana. Route those approvals to Diana from the start so deferred decisions stop stalling deliverables like the business cards.
4
Model fluid products as "core + seasonal." The honey flavors changed constantly and broke the site and GBP sync. A fixed core list with seasonal callouts is the reusable pattern for any product-fluid client.
5
Keep the board current as you go. Closeout statuses were bulk-updated on May 29, so mid-project status was not trustworthy. Same lesson as the internal Sprint 2 retro: move status the moment work changes.
6
Lock decision-maker roles in writing before design starts, and treat post-sign-off changes as a paid extension. Jeannette was set up as the sole design approver, so every choice (brand guidelines, logo, website, honey store) ran through her. At closeout Diana surfaced and rejected most of it: the brand yellow (she called it "baby poop" and wanted it brighter), the honey-store layout (she preferred the old store), and even reversed the core-plus-seasonal flavor model she and Eli had agreed to on a separate call, because they carry many low-supply flavors she still wanted listed. It cost about a one-week extension, extra calls, a full honey-store rebuild toward the old design with a new "special barrel" a-la-carte line, and a photo redo (Jeannette rejected the AI photos that Diana actually preferred). Name the real approvers and their sign-off authority in a written agreement up front. After sign-off, further changes are a paid extension, not free rework.
7
Make operational process-mapping a standard early step, and an offering. This is not unique to SEEDFOLKids. Without mapping the client's operations, who is accountable for what, and who the real decision-makers are, we reach closeout blind to the people who will actually judge the work. Build a lightweight process-mapping step into kickoff. It helps the client organize and it protects our scope, so the right approvers are in the design conversations from day one.
